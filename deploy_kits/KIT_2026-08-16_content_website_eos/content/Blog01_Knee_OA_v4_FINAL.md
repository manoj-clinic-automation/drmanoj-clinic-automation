# Knee Osteoarthritis: Why Knee Pain Doesn't Have to Mean Surgery

*Written and medically reviewed by **Dr. Manoj Agarwal**, MBBS (KGMC Lucknow), MS Orthopaedics (GSVM Kanpur) — Orthopedic & Joint Replacement Surgeon, Bareilly. 30+ years of clinical experience.*

If you have started feeling pain in your knees while climbing stairs, getting up from a chair, or after a long walk, you are not alone — and there is no need to panic. Knee osteoarthritis is one of the most common conditions I see in my OPD. Except in its advanced stages, it is managed well without surgery — and even when surgery does become necessary, modern options give excellent results.

**At a glance**
- Knee OA is common after 45–50 — most patients are managed well without surgery; advanced stages are the exception where surgery genuinely helps
- It is not just "wear and tear" — the joint fluid and lining are involved too, and inflammation plays a role, so lifestyle genuinely helps
- Thigh-muscle exercises and weight control are the two most powerful treatments
- Not every painful knee is OA — gout and other arthritis types need different treatment
- Surgery is the last step on the ladder, never the first — and research on actually regrowing cartilage is advancing fast

**एक नज़र में**
- घुटनों का ऑस्टियोआर्थराइटिस आम है — घबराने की ज़रूरत नहीं; बहुत बढ़ी हुई अवस्था को छोड़कर, ज़्यादातर मरीज़ों का इलाज **बिना ऑपरेशन** होता है
- यह सिर्फ़ "घिसावट" नहीं — घुटने का **चिकनाई वाला द्रव और झिल्ली** भी प्रभावित होते हैं और **सूजन** का हिस्सा होता है, इसलिए नींद, वज़न और खान-पान सुधारना सचमुच फ़ायदा करता है
- **जांघ की मांसपेशियों का व्यायाम** और **वज़न कम करना** सबसे असरदार इलाज हैं
- हर घुटने का दर्द OA नहीं — अचानक लाल-गरम-सूजा घुटना या बुखार हो तो **तुरंत डॉक्टर को दिखाएँ**
- ऑपरेशन आख़िरी कदम है, पहला नहीं — और कार्टिलेज दोबारा उगाने पर शोध तेज़ी से आगे बढ़ रहा है

## What actually happens inside the knee?

*[FIGURE 1 — healthy vs OA knee: cartilage, joint space, synovial fluid labelled]*

Understanding this properly takes away much of the fear, so let me walk you through it the way I explain it in my OPD.

Inside every knee, the bone ends are covered by smooth, cushioning **cartilage**, and the joint is bathed in a slippery **lubricating fluid (synovial fluid)** made by the joint's inner lining. Together they let the knee glide almost friction-free, thousands of times a day.

In osteoarthritis, several things happen slowly, together:

- The **cartilage gradually degrades** — it thins, softens and roughens, losing its cushioning.
- The **joint lining becomes irritated and inflamed**, and the **synovial fluid loses its quality** — it becomes thinner and less lubricating, so the joint no longer glides as smoothly.
- The **bone underneath reacts** — it hardens, and small bony outgrowths (**osteophytes**, or "spurs") form at the edges.
- On a **weight-bearing X-ray**, we see all this indirectly: cartilage itself is invisible on X-ray, so healthy cartilage appears as a clear **gap between the bones**. As cartilage is lost, that **gap narrows** — and we see the degenerative changes (hardened bone, spurs) alongside it. This is exactly why I insist on a *standing* X-ray: it shows the joint under real load.

**And where does the pain come from?** Cartilage itself has no nerve supply — so the pain of OA comes from everything around it: the inflamed joint lining, the stressed bone beneath the thinned cartilage, the stretched capsule, and the overworked muscles trying to protect the joint. That is why pain fluctuates — and why calming inflammation and strengthening muscles genuinely reduces it, even though the years on the joint remain.

### The bone–muscle connection: two silent partners

Two related conditions quietly worsen knee OA, and they deserve your attention:

- **Weakening bones (osteoporosis):** the bone *under* the cartilage is part of the joint's shock-absorbing system. When bone quality falls with age — especially in women after menopause — the joint takes load less well. *(We cover this fully in our osteoporosis guide — link.)*
- **Shrinking muscles (sarcopenia):** from our 50s onward we naturally lose muscle mass unless we actively work to keep it. Weaker thigh muscles mean the knee joint absorbs forces the muscles should have taken — more load, more wear, more pain, and less stability. *(Full guide on age-related muscle loss — link.)*

This is why the treatment of knee OA is never just about the knee — it is about the **bone, the muscle and the load** together.

### And the load itself: your body weight

Here is the arithmetic every patient should know: because of leverage, **every extra kilogram of body weight puts roughly four extra kilograms of force through your knees with each step** — more on stairs. If you are 10 kg above a healthy weight, your knees are absorbing an extra 40 kg of force, thousands of steps a day, year after year. Your knees are quite literally wearing faster because of the extra weight — which also means every kilogram you lose pays you back fourfold.

## Who tends to get it?

Knee OA becomes more common after 45–50 and affects women somewhat more often, particularly after menopause. Risk is higher with excess body weight (the single most important *changeable* factor), a previous knee injury, heavy squatting or kneeling for long hours, or a family history of joint problems.

## What does it feel like?

Pain that worsens with activity and eases with rest; brief morning stiffness that loosens within minutes; a grating sensation (crepitus) on bending; occasional swelling after exertion; a knee that doesn't bend quite as fully as before. Symptoms come and go in phases — a bad week does not mean the joint is "finished."

## Not every painful knee is osteoarthritis

Several other conditions cause knee pain and are treated quite differently:

- **Gout (raised uric acid):** a sudden, intense, hot, red, swollen knee — often striking overnight. It can mimic and even coexist with OA. If your "flare" is unusually fierce and rapid, uric acid is worth checking. *(We offer dedicated [gout treatment](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/gout-treatment/).)*
- **Pseudogout:** a related crystal arthritis in older adults.
- **Rheumatoid arthritis:** symmetrical, several joints, morning stiffness lasting well over half an hour, sometimes fatigue.
- **Spondyloarthritis:** inflammatory arthritis that can settle in one large joint, sometimes with back stiffness or psoriasis.
- **Septic arthritis — a red flag:** a hot, severely painful, swollen knee **with fever** needs **same-day** attention. A joint infection is an emergency.

The reassurance: these are far less common than OA, and a careful examination plus simple blood tests (uric acid, CRP/ESR) — available at the co-located diagnostic lab — usually settle the diagnosis quickly.

## How is it diagnosed?

For straightforward OA: your history, a clinical examination, and a **weight-bearing X-ray** — done in-house on our Fuji digital DR system, so you have the answer the same visit. As explained above, the standing X-ray shows the narrowed joint space and degenerative changes that tell us the stage. An MRI is generally *not* required for routine knee OA. Where the picture looks inflammatory, blood tests separate OA from the conditions above.

## Treatment: we always start gently

**Osteoarthritis is managed in steps, and surgery sits at the very last step, not the first.** Except in advanced stages, the great majority of patients improve substantially without it.

### 1. Physiotherapy and exercise — the real foundation

There is a common fear that using the knee "wears it out faster." The opposite is true. A structured physiotherapy programme is the single most valuable treatment for knee OA:

- **Quadriceps and hip strengthening** — the core of it. Strong thigh muscles take load off the joint with every step and genuinely change the long-term course.
- **Range-of-motion and flexibility** work to keep the knee bending fully.
- **Low-impact conditioning** — stationary cycling and walking programmes that build fitness without pounding the joint.
- **Pain-relief modalities** (TENS, IFT, ultrasound, heat/cold) — honest framing: short-term comfort, *adjuncts* to the strengthening, not the main event.
- **Gait and footwear advice** where needed.
- A **supervised start, then a home programme** — consistency is everything, which is why we run physiotherapy in-house and hand you a routine to continue at home.

### 2. Simple home care: fomentation and knee supports

Two simple measures give real day-to-day comfort when used correctly:

- **Hot fomentation (sikai):** applying warmth — a hot water bag, warm towel, or warm saline fomentation — relaxes the muscles around a *stiff, aching* knee and improves comfort before exercise. Use warmth for stiffness; use a **cold pack** instead when the knee is *swollen or hot* after activity. Never apply extreme heat directly to skin, and diabetics with reduced sensation should be extra careful.
- **Knee supporters and braces:** a simple elastic sleeve gives warmth and a sense of stability for daily activity. A **hinged knee brace** adds side-to-side support for more unstable or deformed knees, and offloading-type braces help selected single-compartment arthritis. Used correctly, a support is a helpful *aid* — but it should never become a substitute for muscle strengthening, because a knee that leans on a brace while the muscles weaken ends up worse. We advise which support suits your knee, and for how long, as part of the treatment plan.

### 3. Weight management

Recall the arithmetic above — every kilogram lost removes roughly four kilograms of force from your knees per step. Nothing else works as reliably over the long term, and it also lowers the metabolic inflammation that drives OA. *(A weight self-check tool is coming to this site; until then, a simple rule: keep your waist under half your height.)*

### 4. Calming the inflammation from within

Because OA has an inflammatory side, daily habits genuinely matter. **Sleep:** poor sleep lowers your pain threshold and raises inflammation — protect it. **Stress:** chronic stress amplifies pain; simple daily practices help more than you'd expect. **Diet:** vegetables, fruit, whole grains, pulses and fermented foods support lower body-wide inflammation; excess sugar, fried and ultra-processed food push the other way. The OA-specific evidence here is still maturing — but this pattern helps your weight and metabolism regardless.

### 5. What about haldi, boswellia and moringa?

Patients ask almost daily, so an honest view: **turmeric/curcumin** — among the better-studied botanicals; trials suggest *standardized extracts* ease pain, but plain kitchen haldi is poorly absorbed, so the benefit comes from standardized preparations. **Boswellia (shallaki)** — also reasonably supported in trials. **Moringa** — nutritious, but good human trials for knee OA are lacking. A caution: supplement quality varies, curcumin and boswellia can mildly thin the blood, and anyone with diabetes, blood pressure, kidney disease or on blood thinners should clear supplements with their doctor first. Adjuncts to exercise and weight control — never replacements.

### 6. Medicines, used sensibly

Pain-relieving tablets help during flare-ups, but they are bridges, not solutions — and unsupervised long-term painkiller use harms the stomach and kidneys, especially with diabetes, blood pressure or kidney disease. Use them under guidance.

### 7. Injections — three different things, honestly explained

"Injection in the knee" means different things, and it helps to know which is which:

- **Steroid (corticosteroid) injection:** settles a badly inflamed, swollen knee quickly. Good for calming a flare — but used sparingly and only under specialist care, because repeated steroid injections can harm cartilage over time.
- **Viscosupplementation (hyaluronic acid — "gel/lubricant injection"):** tops up the thinning joint fluid described earlier. Evidence is mixed — major guidelines are lukewarm — but a proportion of patients with mild-to-moderate OA get months of genuine comfort. We are honest about the odds before you spend on it.
- **PRP (platelet-rich plasma):** your own blood is processed to concentrate the platelets, and the growth-factor-rich portion is injected into the joint. Evidence is growing and several trials show benefit in early-to-moderate OA lasting longer than the alternatives — but results vary between patients and preparations, and it is not a cartilage "regrower." Best suited to selected younger or early-stage knees, decided together after an honest discussion.

None of these reverse arthritis; all are comfort-buying tools within the larger plan.

### 8. Joint-preserving surgery — the step most people don't know exists

For the right patient, there are options *before* replacement. **High tibial osteotomy (HTO):** for younger, active patients whose arthritis affects one compartment with a bow-leg alignment, we realign the limb to shift load off the worn side — preserving your own knee, often for a decade or more, and still convertible to a replacement later. **Proximal fibular osteotomy:** a simpler emerging procedure that helps selected patients in the short term, though the long-term evidence is still limited — an honest conversation decides candidacy.

### 9. Knee replacement — when it is truly warranted

In advanced stages — when the joint space is largely gone, deformity has set in, and pain limits daily life despite a fair trial of everything above — surgery is no longer the enemy but the solution. Modern knee replacement is one of the most successful operations in all of surgery, and most patients regain a comfortable, active life. ## On the horizon: can cartilage be regrown?

For decades the honest answer was no — but this is changing in front of us. In a landmark study published in the journal *Science*, researchers at Stanford Medicine (USA) identified a protein called **15-PGDH** — a "gerozyme" that rises in our bodies as we age and drives tissue degeneration. Blocking this protein **restored lost knee cartilage in aged mice, prevented arthritis from developing after knee injuries, and — remarkably — made severely damaged human cartilage samples (taken during knee replacement surgery) start producing new, functional cartilage in the laboratory**, using the tissue's own cells. An oral version of the drug is already in human trials for age-related muscle weakness.

I share this with realistic hope, not hype: it is not a treatment you can take today, and human joint trials will take years. But it tells us something important — osteoarthritis is moving from a disease we only *manage* toward one we may someday *repair*. All the more reason to protect your knees well in the meantime, so they reach that future in the best possible condition.

## What you can start today

Short regular walks rather than one exhausting one · gentle quadriceps exercises · steady progress toward a healthier weight · hot fomentation for stiffness, cold pack for swelling · protect your sleep · fresh, home-cooked food · supportive footwear · avoid prolonged deep squatting and floor sitting.

## When to see an orthopedic doctor

*[FIGURE 4 — red flags strip]*

- Knee pain persisting beyond two to three weeks
- The knee giving way, locking, or not straightening
- **A hot, red, severely swollen knee — especially with fever (same-day, do not wait)**
- A sudden intense overnight attack (possible gout)
- Pain or swelling in several joints, or morning stiffness lasting over half an hour
- Pain disturbing sleep or stopping daily activities

Early assessment almost always means simpler, gentler treatment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can walking damage my arthritic knee?** No — regular, moderate walking strengthens the muscles that protect the joint. Pace it, don't punish it.

**Do I need an MRI?** Usually not. History, examination and a weight-bearing X-ray diagnose routine knee OA. MRI is reserved for specific situations.

**Should I wear a knee cap or brace all day?** Use supports as advised — typically during activity, not round the clock. A brace helps stability and comfort, but all-day dependence lets the muscles weaken, which worsens the knee long-term. Strengthening remains the real treatment.

**Is the "gel injection" or PRP worth it?** For selected mild-to-moderate knees, yes — some patients get months of real comfort. Evidence is mixed for gel injections and growing for PRP, so we discuss honestly whether *your* knee is a good candidate before you spend.

**Is haldi (turmeric) good for knee pain?** Standardized curcumin extracts have reasonable evidence; plain kitchen haldi is poorly absorbed. Discuss with your doctor before starting any supplement, especially if you have other conditions.

**Will I definitely need a knee replacement one day?** No. Most people with knee OA never reach that point. Replacement is for advanced knees where pain limits daily life despite a fair trial of everything else — and when needed, results are excellent.

## A final word

Knee osteoarthritis is common, well understood, and — except in advanced stages — very manageable without surgery. A diagnosis of OA is not the end of an active life: with the right exercises, attention to weight, bone and muscle health, sleep and diet, and a little patience, most of my patients keep walking, working and living comfortably for many years. And with cartilage-repair research advancing, the future of this disease looks brighter than at any point in my three decades of practice. If your knees have been troubling you, treat it as a reason to act early, not a reason to worry.
