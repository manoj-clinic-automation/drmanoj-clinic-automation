# Low Back Pain: Why Most Backache Gets Better — and the Few Signs That Mean "See a Doctor Now"

*Written and medically reviewed by **Dr. Manoj Agarwal**, MBBS (KGMC Lucknow), MS Orthopaedics (GSVM Kanpur) — Orthopedic & Joint Replacement Surgeon, Bareilly. 30+ years of clinical experience.*

Kamar dard is possibly the most common complaint that walks into my OPD — and also the most misunderstood. So let me say the most important thing first: **the great majority of low back pain is mechanical, comes from muscles, ligaments and discs under strain, and settles with the right care — without surgery, and usually without even an MRI.** A small minority carries warning signs that need urgent attention, and this article will teach you to tell the two apart.

**At a glance**
- Most low back pain is mechanical and settles in days to weeks with the right care
- Bed rest is the old advice — staying gently active heals a back faster
- Core and back-muscle strengthening is the real long-term treatment
- Most backache does not need an MRI — imaging is for specific situations
- A few red flags (explained below) mean same-day medical attention

**एक नज़र में**
- ज़्यादातर कमर दर्द मांसपेशियों और डिस्क पर खिंचाव से होता है और सही देखभाल से **बिना ऑपरेशन** ठीक हो जाता है
- लंबा बेड-रेस्ट पुराना तरीका है — हल्की चाल-फिरत से कमर जल्दी ठीक होती है

## What actually happens in the back?

*[FIGURE 1 — spine segment: vertebrae, disc, nerve root, muscles labelled]*

Your lower back is a stack of bones (**vertebrae**) separated by shock-absorbing cushions (**discs**), held and moved by layers of **muscles and ligaments**, with **nerve roots** exiting between the bones on their way to the legs. Pain can come from any of these — a strained muscle, an overloaded ligament, an irritated disc, a stressed joint between vertebrae — and in most people it is a *combination under mechanical strain*: poor posture, sudden lifting, long sitting, weak supporting muscles, excess body weight.

Two clarifications patients find liberating:
- **Pain intensity does not equal damage.** A severe muscle spasm can hurt far more than a genuinely serious problem. Fear makes back pain worse; understanding calms it.
- **Discs "slipping" is rarely the whole story.** Disc bulges show up on MRIs of many pain-free people too — which is exactly why we treat *the patient*, not the scan. (When a disc genuinely presses a nerve, the picture is different — that is sciatica, covered in its own guide — link.)

**The bone–muscle connection matters here too:** weakening bones (**osteoporosis**) can cause vertebral fractures from trivial strain in older adults — a backache that behaves differently *(dedicated guide — link)* — and shrinking core muscles (**sarcopenia**) remove the spine's natural support jacket *(dedicated guide — link)*. Excess body weight loads the lower back with every step and every hour of sitting.

## What does mechanical back pain feel like?

Aching or stiffness across the lower back, worse with certain movements or after prolonged sitting/standing, better with rest and gentle movement; sometimes spreading to the buttock. It typically improves over days to a few weeks. Pain shooting *down the leg below the knee* points toward nerve involvement — see the sciatica guide.

## Not every backache is "just strain"

- **A fragility fracture:** in an older person — especially a woman past menopause — sudden backache after a trivial bend or lift can be a vertebral fracture from osteoporosis. Needs an X-ray, and bone treatment after.
- **Inflammatory back pain (young adults):** back pain starting *before 40*, creeping on over months, **worse with rest, better with exercise**, with prolonged morning stiffness and night pain — this pattern suggests axial spondyloarthritis and needs a different pathway. It deserves testing, not years of painkillers.
- **Infection:** back pain with fever, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss — urgent evaluation.
- **Referred pain:** kidney stones and some abdominal problems can masquerade as backache.
- **Cauda equina syndrome — an emergency:** see the red flags below.

## How is it diagnosed?

History and a careful clinical examination settle most cases. **Most fresh mechanical back pain needs no imaging at all** in the first weeks — this is honest medicine, not corner-cutting: scans done too early find incidental changes that frighten patients without changing treatment. We image when red flags exist, when pain persists beyond expected, or when nerve signs appear — an X-ray (in-house, digital) for bone questions like fracture; MRI when nerve compression or other specific problems are suspected.

## Treatment: movement is medicine

**1. Stay active — the single biggest myth to bust.** Old advice said "complete bed rest." Modern evidence is emphatic: beyond a day or two of relative rest in a bad spasm, **prolonged bed rest weakens the back and prolongs the pain.** Gentle walking and normal activity, paced to comfort, heal faster.

**2. Physiotherapy and core strengthening — the real foundation.** The spine is held by a muscular jacket — abdominal, back and hip muscles. Strengthening this jacket is what prevents the next episode: guided core work, back extensor strengthening, hip/hamstring flexibility, posture retraining, and a supervised-start-then-home-programme, run through our in-house physiotherapy.

**3. Home care.** Hot fomentation (sikai) for a stiff, spasmed back — warmth relaxes muscle; a cold pack for an acutely inflamed strain in the first day or two. **Lifting technique:** bend the knees, keep the load close, never twist while lifting. **Sitting:** break long sitting every 30–40 minutes; a small lumbar support cushion helps desk workers. Lumbar belts: short-term comfort for heavy-work days — not all-day wear, for the same reason as knee braces: the muscles must do the job.

**4. Weight management.** Every extra kilogram loads the lumbar discs and joints all day long — weight loss is back treatment.

**5. Medicines, used sensibly.** Short courses for flare-ups under guidance; the same caution as always — unsupervised long-term painkillers damage stomach and kidneys.

**6. Injections.** For specific persistent problems (e.g. a confirmed nerve-root inflammation), targeted injections can settle pain and enable rehabilitation — selected cases, decided together.

**7. Surgery — genuinely the exception.** Only for specific structural problems with matching symptoms — significant nerve compression not settling, instability, or emergencies — never for "backache" alone. When truly indicated, outcomes are good. See our [slip disc & spinal conditions](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/slip-discs-spinal-conditions/) and [spine surgery](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/spine-surgeons/) pages.

## What you can start today

Walk daily, gently · begin guided core exercises · fix your lifting technique · break long sitting · hot fomentation for spasm · progress on weight · sleep on a supportive (not sagging) mattress.

## When to see a doctor — and when to RUSH

*[FIGURE 2 — red flags strip]*

**Same-day / emergency (possible cauda equina syndrome):**
- **New difficulty passing or controlling urine or stool**
- **Numbness in the inner thighs / private parts ("saddle" area)**
- **Rapidly progressing weakness in the legs**
These mean severe nerve compression — hours matter.

**Prompt review (days, not months):**
- Back pain with **fever or unexplained weight loss**
- Sudden backache in an **older adult** after minor strain (possible fracture)
- Back pain in a **young adult** that is worse with rest, better with activity, with long morning stiffness
- Pain persisting beyond 3–4 weeks despite sensible care, or any leg weakness/numbness
- History of cancer with new back pain

## Frequently asked questions

**Should I rest in bed until it stops hurting?** No — beyond a day or two, bed rest delays recovery. Gentle movement, paced to comfort, heals faster.

**Do I need an MRI for my back pain?** Usually not in the first weeks. MRI is for red flags, nerve signs, or pain that isn't settling — because early scans often show harmless changes that only cause worry.

**Is a lumbar belt good?** For short-term comfort or heavy-work days, yes. All-day dependence weakens the very muscles that protect your back.

**My pain goes down my leg — is that the same thing?** Pain radiating below the knee suggests nerve involvement (sciatica), which has its own guide and pathway. *(Link.)*

**Can back pain in old age be due to weak bones?** Yes — osteoporotic vertebral fractures are common and under-recognised. Sudden backache after trivial strain in an older person deserves an X-ray and a bone-health check. *(Osteoporosis guide — link.)*

**Which mattress is best?** Medium-firm, not sagging. No single brand is "medical" — support and your comfort matter more than marketing.

## A final word

Most backache is your body asking for stronger muscles, better habits and a little patience — not a sign of something breaking. Learn the red flags, act on them fast if they appear, and otherwise walk, strengthen, and give it time. In three decades I have seen far more backs ruined by fear and inactivity than by exercise.
