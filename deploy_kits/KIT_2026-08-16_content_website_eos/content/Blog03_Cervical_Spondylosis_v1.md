# Cervical Spondylosis: The Truth About "Cervical" — and How to Actually Fix Neck Pain

*Written and medically reviewed by **Dr. Manoj Agarwal**, MBBS (KGMC Lucknow), MS Orthopaedics (GSVM Kanpur) — Orthopedic & Joint Replacement Surgeon, Bareilly. 30+ years of clinical experience.*

"Doctor sahab, mujhe cervical ho gaya hai." I hear this every single day — and the word carries far more fear than the condition deserves. Cervical spondylosis is, in most people, the neck's version of greying hair: age-related change that is extremely common, usually manageable with exercises and habits, and only occasionally serious. Let me explain what it really is, what today's screen-heavy life is doing to our necks, and the few signs that genuinely need urgent attention.

**One word cleared up first:** patients often mix up two similar-sounding terms. **Spondylosis** (this article) is *age-related, degenerative* change — by far the more common. **Spondylitis** is *inflammatory* arthritis of the spine — a different disease with a different treatment path *(our [spondylitis treatment](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/spondylitis-treatment/) page covers it)*. Getting the right label matters, because the treatments differ.

**At a glance**
- Cervical spondylosis = age-related neck change; extremely common after 40, usually well managed without surgery
- Neck exercises and posture correction are the real treatment — collars are only a short-term aid
- Screen habits ("text neck") are loading young necks decades early
- Pain/tingling radiating into the arm means a nerve is irritated — needs assessment
- Rare but serious: clumsy hands, unsteady walking, or bladder change — urgent review

**एक नज़र में**
- "सर्वाइकल" यानी गर्दन के मोहरों में उम्र के साथ बदलाव — 40 के बाद बहुत आम, और ज़्यादातर मरीज़ों का इलाज **बिना ऑपरेशन** होता है
- **गर्दन के व्यायाम और सही posture** ही असली इलाज हैं — पट्टा (collar) सिर्फ़ थोड़े दिनों का सहारा है
- मोबाइल/स्क्रीन झुककर देखने से जवान गर्दनें भी जल्दी घिस रही हैं

## What actually happens in the neck?

*[FIGURE 1 — cervical spine: vertebrae, discs, nerve roots, spinal cord labelled]*

The neck is seven small vertebrae carrying a 4–5 kg head all day, separated by discs, linked by small joints, and — crucially — housing the **spinal cord**, with **nerve roots** exiting at each level toward the shoulders and arms. With age, the discs lose water and height, the small joints roughen, and small bony spurs form — collectively, spondylosis. On an X-ray we see reduced disc spaces and these degenerative changes; most people over 50 show them, **including many with no pain at all** — which is why we treat symptoms, never just reports.

Trouble arises in three patterns: **local neck pain and stiffness** (muscles and joints under strain — the majority); **radiculopathy** (a spur or disc irritating a nerve root → pain, tingling or numbness radiating into the arm/hand along that nerve's path); and, uncommonly, **myelopathy** (the spinal cord itself getting compressed — the serious one, with the red flags below).

**The modern accelerant — screens.** For every inch your head drifts forward over a phone, the effective load on your neck multiplies; a fully bent "texting" neck can load the cervical spine with several times the head's weight, hours a day. This is why I now see spondylosis-type complaints in 25-year-olds. The habit fixes below are not optional extras — they are the treatment.

## What does it feel like?

Neck pain and stiffness, worse after long desk/screen sessions or awkward sleep; pain spreading to the shoulder blades or up to the back of the head (cervicogenic headache); a grating sensation on turning. With nerve-root irritation: sharp pain, tingling or numbness travelling down one arm, sometimes into specific fingers.

## Not every neck pain is spondylosis

Simple muscle strain and stress-related tension are the commonest mimics (and often coexist). **Spondylitis** — inflammatory spinal arthritis, typically younger onset with long morning stiffness — is the important label to separate, as above. Rarely, fever with severe neck stiffness, or neck pain after significant trauma, needs urgent evaluation.

## How is it diagnosed?

History and examination — including checking the nerves of your arms and, importantly, signs of cord involvement — settle most cases. An **X-ray** (in-house, digital) shows the degenerative picture when needed. **MRI is reserved** for significant nerve symptoms, suspected cord compression, or pain not settling — same honest-imaging principle as the back: scan findings alone don't get treated; *you* do.

## Treatment: posture, muscles, patience

**1. Physiotherapy and exercise — the foundation.** **Isometric neck strengthening** (pushing the head against your own palm — front, back, sides — without movement) builds the deep stabilising muscles safely. **Scapular/shoulder-blade strengthening** corrects the rounded-shoulder posture that drives neck strain. Gentle range-of-motion and stretching for tight upper-trapezius muscles. Supervised start in our in-house physiotherapy, then a 10-minute daily home routine — consistency beats intensity.

**2. The habit fixes (this is real treatment):** raise screens to eye level; phone up, not head down; break every 30–40 minutes of desk work with a 30-second neck reset; one thin supportive pillow that keeps the neck level (not two fat ones); avoid reading/phone use lying with the head propped sharply forward.

**3. Home care.** Hot fomentation (sikai) eases muscle spasm beautifully — warm towel or hot water bag, 10–15 minutes, then gentle stretches.

**4. About the cervical collar — honest words.** A soft collar can rest an acutely painful neck for a *few days*. Worn for weeks, it does the opposite of healing: the neck muscles weaken and stiffness worsens. If you have been living in a collar for months, that is the first thing we will gently take off.

**5. Traction — selected cases.** Intermittent cervical traction, properly done in physiotherapy, relieves selected nerve-root irritation — a tool for specific situations, not a routine for every neck.

**6. Medicines** — short flare courses under guidance; nerve-pain-specific medicines for radiculopathy where appropriate. The usual caution against long unsupervised painkiller use stands.

**7. Surgery — uncommon, for clear reasons only.** Reserved mainly for significant or progressing nerve compression, and for myelopathy — where timely surgery protects the spinal cord. "Cervical spondylosis" on a report is never by itself a reason to operate. *(Our [spine care](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/spine-surgeons/) page covers the surgical side.)*

## What you can start today

Screen to eye level · 30-second neck breaks every half hour · isometric neck exercises morning and evening · one good pillow · sikai for stiffness · shoulders back, gently, a hundred times a day until it becomes you.

## When to see a doctor — and when it's urgent

*[FIGURE 2 — red flags strip]*

**Urgent (possible spinal cord involvement — myelopathy):**
- **Hands becoming clumsy** — buttons, writing, dropping objects
- **Unsteady, imbalanced walking**
- **Weakness in arms or legs**, or **new bladder/bowel disturbance**

**Prompt review:**
- Pain, tingling or numbness radiating down an arm
- Neck pain with fever, or after significant injury
- Pain not settling after 3–4 weeks of sensible care
- Long morning stiffness in a younger person (possible inflammatory spondylitis — different pathway)

## Frequently asked questions

**Is "cervical" curable?** The age-related changes don't reverse — but the *pain* is very treatable, and most patients live comfortably with exercises and habits. Think "managed excellently," like greying hair you keep healthy.

**Should I wear a cervical collar daily?** No. A few days in an acute episode at most. Long-term collar use weakens the neck and worsens the problem.

**Which pillow is right?** One medium, supportive pillow keeping the neck in line with the spine. Neither a stack of pillows nor none.

**Are neck-cracking (chiropractic-style) manipulations safe?** Forceful neck manipulation carries real, though rare, risks and I don't recommend it — especially with nerve symptoms or significant spondylosis. Guided exercise achieves the goal safely.

**My arm tingles — is that dangerous?** It means a nerve root is irritated. Most cases settle with conservative care, but it needs proper assessment first — please don't just take painkillers for months.

**Can youngsters get this?** The changes are age-related, but screen posture is bringing neck strain — and early change — to the young. The habit fixes above are the prevention.

## A final word

Your neck has carried your head faithfully for decades — some wear is honourable, not alarming. Strengthen it, position your screens as if your neck sends you the bill, respect the few red flags, and cervical spondylosis becomes a condition you manage in minutes a day rather than one that manages you.
