# Sciatica: When the Leg Hurts but the Problem Is in the Back

*Written and medically reviewed by **Dr. Manoj Agarwal**, MBBS (KGMC Lucknow), MS Orthopaedics (GSVM Kanpur) — Orthopedic & Joint Replacement Surgeon, Bareilly. 30+ years of clinical experience.*

Few pains confuse patients more than this one: the *leg* hurts — shooting, burning, tingling down the thigh and calf — yet massage, oils and leg treatments do nothing, because the problem is not in the leg at all. It is a nerve being irritated in the lower back. That is sciatica. And here is the reassurance to hold onto from the start: **most sciatica settles with conservative care over weeks, without surgery** — while a couple of rare situations are true emergencies you must be able to recognise.

**At a glance**
- Sciatica is nerve pain travelling from the back down the leg — usually a disc pressing a nerve root
- The leg hurts, but the treatment targets the back — leg massage won't fix it
- Most cases improve substantially within 6–12 weeks with conservative care
- MRI is for persistent or worsening cases, not day one
- Bladder/bowel change or saddle numbness with sciatica = EMERGENCY, hours matter

## What actually happens?

*[FIGURE 1 — lumbar spine with disc pressing nerve root; sciatic nerve path down the leg highlighted]*

From your lower back, nerve roots exit the spine and join to form the **sciatic nerve** — the body's thickest nerve — running through the buttock, down the back of the thigh, and branching into the calf and foot. When something irritates a nerve root at its origin — most commonly a **bulging or herniated disc** pressing on it, with the chemical inflammation that accompanies it — the pain is felt **along the nerve's whole territory** in the leg, even though the leg itself is perfectly healthy. That is why the pain often runs *below the knee*, may come with tingling, numbness or weakness in a specific patch of the leg or foot, and often worsens with sitting, bending, coughing or sneezing.

Age changes the usual cause: in the **young and middle-aged**, a disc herniation is the typical culprit; in **older adults**, narrowing of the nerve canal (**spinal stenosis**) is common — classically leg pain on *walking* that eases on sitting or bending forward. **Piriformis-type buttock problems** can mimic sciatica by irritating the nerve in the buttock itself. The examination tells these apart.

**And the reassuring biology:** a herniated disc fragment is not a permanent stone — the body gradually shrinks and resorbs much of it over weeks to months, which is precisely why most sciatica improves *without* anyone operating. Time, done right, is a genuine treatment.

## What does it feel like?

Sharp, shooting, burning or electric pain from the buttock down one leg, often below the knee; tingling or numbness in parts of the leg or foot; sometimes weakness (foot slapping, toe-drag); worse with sitting, forward bending, coughing/sneezing. Usually one-sided. Back pain may or may not accompany it — some patients have leg symptoms alone.

## Not every leg pain is sciatica

Knee arthritis pain stays around the knee *(knee guide — link)*. Blood-supply problems cause calf pain on walking relieved by standing still (different from stenosis, which needs sitting/bending). Simple muscle cramps, hamstring strains and hip problems have their own patterns. And diabetic nerve damage causes burning in *both* feet in a "sock" pattern — a different disease. Examination separates these.

## How is it diagnosed?

History and examination — straight-leg-raise testing, and mapping exactly which nerve's territory the symptoms follow, plus checking muscle power and reflexes — diagnose sciatica clinically. **MRI is not a day-one test:** since most cases settle, we scan when red flags appear, when weakness is significant or progressing, or when 4–6 weeks of proper care hasn't turned the corner — because that is when the result would actually change the plan. An X-ray (in-house) answers bone questions where relevant.

## Treatment: patience with a plan

**1. Stay moving, wisely.** As with back pain, prolonged bed rest slows recovery. Short rest in the worst first days, then gentle walking and position changes as comfort allows. Avoid the specific aggravators for now — long sitting, deep forward bending, heavy lifting.

**2. Physiotherapy — the structured path.** **Nerve-gliding ("nerve flossing") exercises** gently mobilise the irritated nerve; **directional/extension-based exercises** (chosen after assessment — not one-size-fits-all) reduce pressure on the root; then progressive **core strengthening** protects the back long-term. Supervised start in our in-house physiotherapy, graduating to a home routine.

**3. Home care.** Hot fomentation on the lower back for the accompanying muscle spasm; short walks several times a day rather than long sitting; a firm-supportive sleeping surface; when sitting is unavoidable, upright with lumbar support and frequent breaks.

**4. Medicines, used sensibly.** Beyond ordinary short-course painkillers, **nerve-pain-specific medicines** genuinely help many patients through the irritated-nerve phase — prescribed and adjusted by a doctor, not self-taken. The standing caution on unsupervised long-term painkillers applies doubly here, because sciatica tempts people into months of tablets.

**5. Targeted injections.** For persistent nerve-root inflammation, an image-guided injection around the root can settle pain enough to let rehabilitation proceed — a selected-case tool, decided together.

**6. Surgery — for the few, and then it works well.** Indications are specific: **progressive or significant muscle weakness**, the **emergency below**, or disabling pain that has genuinely not responded to weeks of proper conservative care. For a clear disc herniation matching the symptoms, surgery relieves leg pain reliably — but it is the last chapter, not the first. *(Our [slip disc & spinal conditions](https://drmanojagarwal.com/services/slip-discs-spinal-conditions/) page covers this.)*

## What you can start today

Gentle short walks, several times daily · avoid long sitting and deep bending for now · sikai for back spasm · learn the nerve-glide exercise properly, then do it consistently · resist the urge to get "leg massage" — kindly, it's aiming at the wrong address.

## When to see a doctor — and when to RUSH

*[FIGURE 2 — red flags strip]*

**EMERGENCY — go the same day (possible cauda equina syndrome):**
- **New difficulty passing or controlling urine or stool**
- **Numbness in the saddle area** (inner thighs, private parts)
- **Rapidly worsening leg weakness or numbness in both legs**

**Prompt review:**
- Foot weakness — toes dragging, foot slapping while walking
- Sciatica with fever, unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer
- Pain not meaningfully better after 4–6 weeks of proper care
- Recurrent episodes — worth a definitive assessment and prevention plan

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my leg hurt if the problem is in my back?** The nerve that supplies your leg starts in your back. Pressing a wire at the switchboard makes the bulb flicker — the fault is at the board, not the bulb.

**Will massage or oils cure sciatica?** They may relax muscles and soothe temporarily, but they cannot decompress a nerve root in the spine. The effective treatments work on the back.

**How long does sciatica take to go?** Most patients improve substantially in 6–12 weeks with proper care. Improvement is usually gradual — leg pain typically recedes toward the back before fading.

**Do I need an MRI immediately?** No — unless red flags or significant weakness are present. We scan when the result would change the plan.

**Does sciatica always need surgery?** No — the large majority never do. Surgery is for progressive weakness, emergencies, or genuinely failed conservative care, and in those cases it works well.

**Can sciatica come back?** It can, which is why the core-strengthening and habit phase after recovery is not optional — it is the insurance.

## A final word

Sciatica is frightening because the pain is fierce and travels — but its biology is on your side: most compressed nerves recover as the body itself shrinks the offending disc material. Give it structured care, respect the red flags without fearing every twinge, and let patience do its quiet work. The leg that brought you in limping usually walks out on its own strength.
